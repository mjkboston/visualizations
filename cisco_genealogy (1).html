The mechanism

Cisco engineers depart freely under California §16600, found competing companies, grow them, and Cisco either acquires them (Sourcefire $2.7B, Meraki $1.2B, Splunk $28B) or competes against them (Juniper, Palo Alto Networks, Aruba, Nile). Each departure seeds the next generation. Each acquisition brings talent back into Cisco, which then departs again. The flywheel never stops.

The Massachusetts contrast

The Route 128 networking cluster (Proteon, Interlan, Wellfleet, Cabletron, Cascade, Bay Networks, Sycamore, Starent) operated in the same era, in the same industry, with comparable talent. But Massachusetts non-compete law meant each departure required navigation rather than automatic legal freedom. The chain terminated; the California chain compounds still.

The CEO who competed with his own company. In 2018, John Chambers — who had run Cisco for 20 years — departed and co-founded Nile, a startup competing directly with Cisco in enterprise networking. He said: "We're building something that our prior company was not building." California §16600 made this legally automatic. In Massachusetts, a departing CEO founding a direct competitor would face non-compete scrutiny, require garden leave payment, or need an explicit contractual waiver. In California, no such barrier existed. Chambers raised $300M+ and is actively competing with his former company.

Key chains in the Cisco genealogy

1

Cisco → Juniper → Fungible → Microsoft

Pradeep Sindhu left Xerox PARC (not Cisco directly) to found Juniper (1996) as a direct Cisco competitor. After Juniper's IPO and growth, Sindhu left to found Fungible (2015, data processing units), acquired by Microsoft. Three California departures, three companies, each legally automatic under §16600.

2

Cisco → Meraki ($1.2B) → Samsara (NYSE: IOT, $20B)

Biswas and Bicket founded Meraki from MIT research — choosing San Francisco over Boston. Cisco acquired for $1.2B (2012). Founders departed to found Samsara (2015), which went public at $20B market cap. This is the Cisco flywheel at its most complete: departure → founding → acquisition → departure → founding → IPO.

3

Cisco → Aruba ($3B) → Mist → Juniper ($405M) → HPE

Melkote (ex-Cisco, ex-Alteon) founded Aruba (2002), acquired by HP ($3B, 2015). Ex-Cisco/Aruba engineers including Hajela (who had led Cisco's Meraki acquisition) founded Mist Systems (2014), acquired by Juniper ($405M, 2019), now part of HPE's $14B Juniper acquisition. The chain: Cisco → Aruba → Mist → Juniper → HPE.

4

Cisco → Palo Alto Networks ($100B+)

Nir Zuk (Check Point → NetScreen → Juniper after NetScreen $4B acquisition) founded PAN in 2005 with Rajiv Batra (ex-Juniper VP Engineering). Every step was legally automatic. PAN is now the world's largest standalone cybersecurity company at $9.2B in revenue and $100B+ market cap — founded by engineers departing California employers to compete with those employers.

5

Cisco CEO → Nile (competing with Cisco)

John Chambers ran Cisco for 20 years. After retirement, he co-founded Nile (2018) with Pankaj Patel (ex-Cisco CDO) to compete with Cisco in enterprise networking. California §16600 made this legally automatic — no non-compete, no garden leave, no litigation risk. Nile has raised $300M+ and is actively competing with Cisco. The CEO who built Cisco is now its competitor.
