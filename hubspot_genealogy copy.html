HubSpot: The Orange Mafia

Cambridge, MA · 2006. Two MIT graduate students coined "inbound marketing" and built Boston's first true "platform company" — one that spawns startups rather than consuming them. 72 alumni companies. $740M raised. And the 2018 reform that made it possible. Click any node to explore.

The founding

Brian Halligan (ex-PTC SVP Sales, MIT MBA) and Dharmesh Shah (ex-Pyramid Digital CEO, MIT MS) met at MIT Sloan in 2004 and founded HubSpot in June 2006. They coined "inbound marketing" — the insight that the internet had shifted power to buyers, and that helping customers find you was more effective than interrupting them. From $255K in year-one revenue to $2.6 billion in 2024.

The platform company

HubSpot is the first Massachusetts technology company since the minicomputer era that can credibly be called a "platform company" — one that trains talent at scale and blesses departures to found new companies. Underscore VC named HubSpot explicitly as Boston's model for how iconic companies should behave. The 72-company HubSpot Mafia is the evidence.

2010: Halligan defends non-competes. In a 2010 interview, CEO Brian Halligan said: "One thing that I think is unique to Boston relative to the West Coast is that people are far more loyal... Non-competes are good because they make loyalty contractual." This is the collective action problem personified — a founder who benefited from mobility (leaving PTC to found HubSpot) defending the legal structures that impeded it for his own employees.
After 2018: The "blessed departure" culture emerges. The 2018 Massachusetts Non-Compete Act shifted the legal landscape enough that HubSpot's culture could follow. Halligan and Shah began actively investing in alumni companies. The Culture Code (2013) had already established departure-as-aspiration. Post-reform, that aspiration became legally accessible. The HubSpot Mafia is the result — 72 companies, Boston's first evidence of Fairchild-style compounding.
