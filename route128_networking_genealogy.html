Route 128's Internet Layer: The Networking Cluster That Built the Web's Backbone — and Then Disappeared

MIT/ARPANET (1969) through Starent/Cisco (2009). Massachusetts produced Proteon, Interlan, Wellfleet, Cabletron, Cascade, Bay Networks, Sycamore, and Starent — a cluster of world-class networking companies. All were acquired. None generated a successor ecosystem. Cisco, founded the same year as Wellfleet, is worth $240 billion and spawned Juniper, Palo Alto Networks, and dozens more.

The cluster

From MIT's ARPANET node in 1969 through Starent's $2.9B Cisco acquisition in 2009, Massachusetts produced a generation of networking companies that built real infrastructure — routers, switches, ATM gear, optical networks, mobile packet core. Proteon built the first commercial multi-protocol router. Wellfleet was Cisco's closest competitor. Cascade transformed telecoms. Sycamore pioneered optical networking. Starent built 4G mobile infrastructure.

The pattern

Every major Massachusetts networking company was eventually acquired. None generated a successor ecosystem. The knowledge that built Bay Networks dispersed into Nortel, which went bankrupt. The knowledge that built Cascade dispersed into Ascend, then Lucent, then Agere. Deshpande's Sycamore, which peaked at $18 billion, became Coriant, then Infinera. The chain compounded — and then terminated. In California, the equivalent chain is still running.

Cabletron's history names the mechanism explicitly. When Cabletron moved from Massachusetts to New Hampshire in 1985, its founders discovered that "lower housing mortgages attracted well-trained, highly creative software and hardware engineers from the failing computer businesses situated along Route 128." DEC and Wang engineers — constrained by non-compete agreements from founding their own companies — relocated across the state border and went to work for Cabletron instead. The talent circulated within the ecosystem rather than spinning off into new ventures. This is the non-compete constraint made visible in a single sentence of corporate history.

The Deshpande chain — three companies, three exits, one serial founder

1

Coral Networks (1988) → sold to SynOptics for $15M

Deshpande arrived in Massachusetts from Canada in 1984. Within four years he had co-founded Coral Networks, a router developer. He departed before its SynOptics sale and used the proceeds to fund the next venture.

2

Cascade Communications (1990) → sold to Ascend for $3.7B

Cascade built ATM and frame relay switches for telecommunications companies handling the internet's explosive data growth. Deshpande and CEO Dan Smith grew it to be the dominant ATM switching vendor. Cascade alumni went on to found multiple Boston-area telecom companies in the late 1990s — the cluster's best documented second-generation effect.

3

Sycamore Networks (1998) → $18B peak valuation → Coriant → Infinera

Deshpande and Smith immediately re-founded after the Cascade sale, building intelligent optical networking equipment. Sycamore went public in 1999, peaked at $18 billion, then collapsed with the dot-com bust. It was eventually merged into Coriant (2013) and then acquired by Infinera — a California company. The Deshpande chain terminated in Massachusetts even as it peaked there.

4

Starent Networks (2000) → sold to Cisco for $2.9B

Founded by Cascade/NetCore alums Ashraf Dahod and Anthony Schoener. Built IP-based mobile packet core for 3G/4G networks. Acquired by Cisco in 2009 — the last significant exit in the Massachusetts networking cluster. The technology went to California; the engineers went to work for Cisco.
