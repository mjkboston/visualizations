The PayPal Mafia: Modern Fairchild

1998–2002. 220 employees. $1.5B acquisition by eBay. Culture clash. Mass dispersal. Result: Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube, Palantir, Founders Fund, Sequoia's next chapter. All legally uninhibited by California §16600. Click any node to explore.

The dispersal event

When eBay acquired PayPal in October 2002, its 220 employees found the corporate culture completely incompatible with PayPal's autonomous, high-urgency environment. Within four years, every PayPal employee had departed. They stayed connected — emailing, investing in each other, sharing deals. What they built is worth multiple trillions. PayPal itself, the company that stayed, is worth approximately $40 billion.

The California foundation

Not one of the 220 departing PayPal employees faced a non-compete agreement preventing them from founding competitors in online payments, consumer internet, space, or electric vehicles. California §16600 voided such agreements by statute. The same legal freedom that enabled Fairchild's departures in 1957 enabled PayPal's dispersal in 2002 — and the compounding principle operated identically.

The ratio that defines the non-compete argument. PayPal was acquired for $1.5 billion in 2002. PayPal today is worth approximately $40 billion — a 27× increase over 24 years. Its departed employees built companies worth multiple trillions. Tesla alone is worth $1.3T+. Palantir is $400B. Meta (Thiel's early bet) is $1.4T. LinkedIn sold for $26.2B. The ratio of departed-employee value to retained-company value is roughly 25:1. That ratio is the non-compete argument in a single number: what leaves is worth far more than what stays.
